3.1 Probability Distributions

Understand discrete random variables and construct probability distribution tables.

🎯 Quick Decision Challenge

Which Game Would You Choose?

Before learning the definition, imagine that you are choosing between two simple games. Look at the possible rewards first — but do not calculate anything yet.

Game A

You could win RM2, RM5 or RM10.

Game B

You could also win RM2, RM5 or RM10.

Which game is better?
You know the possible rewards, but that is still not enough to make a good decision.

💡 What information is missing?

We also need to know how likely each possible reward is. A probability distribution connects every possible value of a random variable with its corresponding probability.

1️⃣ Define \(X\)
2️⃣ List possible values
3️⃣ Assign probabilities
4️⃣ Build the distribution
Discrete Random Variable

A discrete random variable is a random variable which takes a fixed number of countable values.

The probability distribution of a discrete random variable \( X \) is a list of all possible values of the random variable \( X \) and their corresponding probabilities.

Example:The number of defective sensors detected during a quality check.

Rolling Two Dice: Counting Sixes

Two fair dice are rolled once. Let \(X\) denote the number of dice that show a six. Construct the probability distribution of \(X\).

💡

Step 1: Identify the possible values of \(X\).

With two dice, the number of sixes can only be 0, 1 or 2.

💡

Step 2: Find the probability for each value.

For each die, \(P(\text{six})=\frac16\) and \(P(\text{not six})=\frac56\).

Solution

\(X\)012
\(P(X=x)\)\(\frac{25}{36}\)\(\frac{10}{36}\)\(\frac{1}{36}\)
\[ P(X=0)=\left(\frac56\right)^2=\frac{25}{36},\quad P(X=1)=2\left(\frac16\right)\left(\frac56\right)=\frac{10}{36},\quad P(X=2)=\left(\frac16\right)^2=\frac{1}{36} \]
⭐ CHECK

The probabilities add to 1: \(\frac{25}{36}+\frac{10}{36}+\frac{1}{36}=1\).

Properties of Discrete Probability Distributions

Every probability distribution must satisfy two mathematical rules:

ΣP
FORMULA

Sum of Probabilities

\[ \sum P(X=x) = 1 \]
≤1
FORMULA

Valid Range

\[ 0 \le P(X=x) \le 1 \]

A discrete random variable \(X\) has probability function

\[ P(X=x)=kx,\qquad x=1,2,3,4 \]

(a) Determine the value of the constant \(k\).

💡

Think First

Which probability-distribution property allows us to determine \(k\)?

Hint: The probabilities must sum to 1.

\[ \sum P(X=x)=1 \]

Solution (a)

\[ \begin{aligned} k(1)+k(2)+k(3)+k(4)&=1\\ 10k&=1\\ k&=\frac{1}{10} \end{aligned} \]

(b) Find \(P(X\ge 3)\).

💡 Think First

Which values of \(X\) satisfy \(X\ge3\)?

\[ \begin{aligned} P(X\ge3)&=P(X=3)+P(X=4)\\ &=3\left(\frac{1}{10}\right)+4\left(\frac{1}{10}\right)\\ &=\frac{7}{10}=0.7 \end{aligned} \]

3.2 Mean, Variance & Expectation

Find the expected value, variance and standard deviation of a discrete random variable.

🎯 Quick Decision Challenge

Same Average — Same Choice?

Imagine that you can choose one of two reward games. Both games have the same average reward, but they behave very differently. Which one would you prefer?

Game A — Stable

You receive RM5 every time.

Possible reward: RM5 only.

Game B — Risky

You receive RM0 with probability 0.5 or RM10 with probability 0.5.

Possible rewards: RM0 or RM10.

Both games have mean reward RM5. Are they really the same?
Your choice tells us something important: the average is useful, but it does not describe everything about a distribution.

💡 Why do these games feel different?

They have the same mean, but Game B is much more spread out. To describe a probability distribution properly, we often need both its centre and its variability.

Game A
Mean = RM5
Variance = 0
Game B
Mean = RM5
Variance = 25

⭐ Big idea: The mean / expected value tells us the long-run centre, while the variance tells us how much the possible values spread around that centre.

Important Formulas
μ
FORMULA

Mean / Expectation

\[ \mu = E(X) = \sum xP(X=x) \]
σ²
FORMULA

Variance

\[ \sigma^2 = \sum x^2 P(X=x) - \mu^2 \]
σ
FORMULA

Standard Deviation

\[ \sigma = \sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(X)} \]

A discrete random variable \(X\) has the probability distribution below. Find the mean, variance and standard deviation.

\(X\)0123
\(P(X=x)\)0.100.300.400.20

💡 Hint 1

Start with \(E(X)=\sum xP(X=x)\).

💡 Hint 2

Then calculate \(E(X^2)=\sum x^2P(X=x)\).

💡 Hint 3

Use \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2\).

Solution Steps

\[ \begin{aligned} E(X)&=0(0.10)+1(0.30)+2(0.40)+3(0.20)=1.70\\[3pt] E(X^2)&=0^2(0.10)+1^2(0.30)+2^2(0.40)+3^2(0.20)=3.70\\[3pt] \operatorname{Var}(X)&=3.70-(1.70)^2=0.81\\[3pt] \sigma&=\sqrt{0.81}=0.90 \end{aligned} \]

The probability distribution of \(X\) is given below:

\(X\)01234
\(P(X=x)\)0.15C0.250.200.10

Determine \(C\), then find \(E(X)\).

💡 Think First

Before calculating \(E(X)\), use the fact that all probabilities sum to 1.

Solve \(0.15+C+0.25+0.20+0.10=1\).

\[ 0.70+C=1\quad\Rightarrow\quad C=0.30 \] \[ \begin{aligned} E(X)&=0(0.15)+1(0.30)+2(0.25)+3(0.20)+4(0.10)\\ &=1.80 \end{aligned} \]
📝 Expected Monetary Value

A sales representative receives an RM80 commission from a special promotion with probability 0.08. If no commission is earned, an RM3 administrative cost is incurred. Let \(X\) be the net monetary outcome. Find \(E(X)\) and interpret the result.

\[ \begin{aligned} E(X)&=(80)(0.08)+(-3)(0.92)\\ &=6.40-2.76\\ &=3.64 \end{aligned} \]
🧠 Interpret Your Answer

Over many similar promotions, the expected net outcome is a gain of RM3.64 per promotion.

From Frequency Data to a Probability Distribution

Apply the ideas of probability distribution, mean and variance in one guided example.

📊 From Frequency Data to Probability Distribution

Campus Café Orders

A campus café records the number of drinks ordered in 20 randomly selected transactions. The results are shown below. Let \(X\) represent the number of drinks ordered in one transaction.

Number of drinks, \(x\) 1 2 3 4
Number of transactions 8 6 4 2
Tasks
  1. Construct the probability distribution table of \(X\).
  2. Find the mean of \(X\) and interpret the result.
  3. Find the variance of \(X\).
(a) Construct the probability distribution table of \(X\)

💡 Hint 1

Add all frequencies first to find the total number of transactions.

💡 Hint 2

Use \(P(X=x)=\dfrac{\text{frequency}}{\text{total frequency}}\) for each value of \(x\).

\[ \text{Total frequency}=8+6+4+2=20 \]
\(X\) 1 2 3 4
\(P(X=x)\) \(\frac{8}{20}=0.40\) \(\frac{6}{20}=0.30\) \(\frac{4}{20}=0.20\) \(\frac{2}{20}=0.10\)
✓ Check

\(0.40+0.30+0.20+0.10=1\).

(b) Find the mean of \(X\) and interpret the result

💡 Hint 1

Use the probability distribution from part (a) and calculate \(E(X)=\sum xP(X=x)\).

💡 Hint 2

Multiply each \(x\)-value by its probability, then add the products.

\[ \begin{aligned} E(X) &=1(0.40)+2(0.30)+3(0.20)+4(0.10)\\ &=0.40+0.60+0.60+0.40\\ &=2.00 \end{aligned} \]
🧠 Interpretation

Over many similar transactions, the café can expect an average of 2 drinks per transaction.

(c) Find the variance of \(X\)

💡 Hint 1

First calculate \(E(X^2)=\sum x^2P(X=x)\).

💡 Hint 2

Then use \(\operatorname{Var}(X)=E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2\), with the mean from part (b).

\[ \begin{aligned} E(X^2) &=1^2(0.40)+2^2(0.30)+3^2(0.20)+4^2(0.10)\\ &=0.40+1.20+1.80+1.60\\ &=5.00 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(X) &=E(X^2)-[E(X)]^2\\ &=5.00-(2.00)^2\\ &=1.00 \end{aligned} \]

3.3 Binomial Distribution

Use the binomial distribution as a model for solving problems.

🎯 Decision Challenge

Would You Be Worried?

Before learning any formula, make a judgement from a real situation. There is no penalty for your choice — the purpose is to make you think.

🏭 A factory normally produces a defective item with probability 0.05. Today, a quality manager randomly inspects 10 items and finds 3 defective items.

What would you conclude?
Good — you have made a prediction. But a judgement alone does not tell us how unusual 3 defective items really are.

🤔 So how can we know?

We need a way to calculate the probability of getting 3 or more defective items out of 10 when the defect probability is 0.05. This is exactly the type of uncertainty that Binomial Distribution can model.

📌 Remember your first judgement. After learning the topic, you should be able to return to this situation and make a more informed decision using probability rather than intuition alone.
🌍 Why This Matters

Why Are We Learning Binomial Distribution?

Binomial Distribution is not just another formula in Statistics. It is a way to model repeated yes/no outcomes, measure uncertainty, and support decisions in real situations. Whenever we repeatedly observe an outcome such as success/failure, respond/not respond, defective/not defective or pass/fail, a binomial model may be useful if its assumptions are satisfied.

What is happening?
2️⃣ RECOGNISE

Real-Life Applications of Binomial Distribution

Click each situation. Focus first on the question being answered, then notice how the real situation becomes a binomial model.

Suppose 20 components are inspected and each component has probability \(p=0.03\) of being defective.
\(X\): number of defective components
Model: \(X \sim B(20,0.03)\)
Why it matters: The calculated probability can help a quality team judge whether the observed number of defects is unusual and whether further investigation may be needed.
Suppose 12 patients independently receive a treatment and the response probability is \(p=0.70\).
\(X\): number of patients who respond
Model: \(X \sim B(12,0.70)\)
Why it matters: The model can quantify how likely different response counts are under the stated probability.
Suppose a campaign is sent to 30 independently selected customers and each has probability \(p=0.20\) of responding.
\(X\): number of customers who respond
Model: \(X \sim B(30,0.20)\)
Why it matters: The probability distribution helps describe the range of response counts that could reasonably occur.
Suppose 15 similar independent components are used and each has failure probability \(p=0.05\).
\(X\): number of failed components
Model: \(X \sim B(15,0.05)\)
Why it matters: The model can quantify the probability of seeing zero, one or several failures under the assumed conditions.
Suppose 25 students independently answer a particular item and each has probability \(p=0.60\) of answering correctly.
\(X\): number of correct responses
Model: \(X \sim B(25,0.60)\)
Why it matters: The model illustrates how probability can describe the expected spread of correct-response counts across a class.
Suppose 10 independently observed visitors each have purchase probability \(p=0.30\).
\(X\): number of visitors who purchase
Model: \(X \sim B(10,0.30)\)
Why it matters: The binomial model converts an uncertain customer outcome into probabilities for different purchase counts.
⭐ BIG IDEA

Binomial Distribution is a decision-support model for repeated binary outcomes. The calculation is important, but the deeper skill is recognising the situation, defining the random variable, checking the assumptions, and interpreting what the probability means in context.

🧠 Quick Appreciation Check

A factory inspects 10 items and records whether each item is defective or not. Why might Binomial Distribution be useful?

Because the variable of interest can be the number of defective items among a fixed number of inspections. If the inspections are appropriately independent and the defect probability is approximately constant, the number of defects can be modelled using a binomial distribution. The resulting probabilities help describe how likely different defect counts are.
3.3 · BINOMIAL DISTRIBUTION

Requirements, Formula, Mean & Variance

Check the binomial requirements, then learn the probability, mean and variance formulas.

Requirements & Formula

A binomial experiment satisfies 4 requirements:

  1. Fixed number of trials (\( n \)).
  2. Only 2 possible outcomes (Success or Failure).
  3. Independent trials.
  4. Constant probability of success (\( p \)).
B
FORMULA

Binomial Probability Formula

\[ X \sim B(n, p) \]
\[ P(X=r) = \binom{n}{r} p^r q^{n-r} \]
\[ q = 1 - p \]
\( n = \) number of trials
\( p = \) probability of success
\( q = \) probability of failure
\( r = \) number of successes
Mean & Variance of Binomial Distribution
μ
FORMULA

Mean

\[ \mu = np \]
σ²
FORMULA

Variance

\[ \sigma^2 = npq \]
📘 Guided Example

Online Learning Satisfaction

A survey shows that 70% of students are satisfied with an online learning platform. A random sample of 12 students is selected.

Calculate the mean, variance and standard deviation of the number of students who are:
(a) satisfied with the platform
(b) not satisfied with the platform
(a) Students who are satisfied

💡 Hint 1 — Identify the parameters

Let \(X\) be the number of satisfied students. Here, \(n=12\), \(p=0.70\), and \(q=1-p=0.30\).

💡 Hint 2 — Choose the formulas

Use \(\mu=np\), \(\sigma^2=npq\), and \(\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^2}\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu &= np\\ &=12(0.70)\\ &=8.4 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma^2 &= npq\\ &=12(0.70)(0.30)\\ &=2.52 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma &= \sqrt{\sigma^2}\\ &=\sqrt{2.52}\\ &\approx1.587 \end{aligned} \]
✓ Part (a)

Mean \(=8.4\), variance \(=2.52\), standard deviation \(\approx1.587\).

(b) Students who are not satisfied

💡 Hint 1 — Change the meaning of success

Let \(Y\) be the number of students who are not satisfied. Now the probability of success is \(p=0.30\), so \(q=0.70\).

💡 Hint 2 — Apply the same formulas

Use \(\mu=np\), \(\sigma^2=npq\), and \(\sigma=\sqrt{\sigma^2}\) with \(p=0.30\) and \(q=0.70\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \mu &= np\\ &=12(0.30)\\ &=3.6 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma^2 &= npq\\ &=12(0.30)(0.70)\\ &=2.52 \end{aligned} \]
\[ \begin{aligned} \sigma &= \sqrt{\sigma^2}\\ &=\sqrt{2.52}\\ &\approx1.587 \end{aligned} \]
✓ Part (b)

Mean \(=3.6\), variance \(=2.52\), standard deviation \(\approx1.587\).
